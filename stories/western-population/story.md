How many Westerners lived in the Indonesian archipelago, where, and what made the number rise and fall? The answer depends on a colonial legal category, "Europeans", which covered Dutch-born newcomers (totok), a much larger group born in the Indies, and some Asians and other foreigners granted the status. This story follows the published counts, says where they differ, and uses modern provinces as stand-ins for colonial regions on the map. Sea routes that leave the frame towards the Bay of Biscay continue to the Netherlands.

Markus Vink's census of the Dutch Indian Ocean world finds the European group in Batavia stagnant or declining. He counts 1,783 Europeans in 1699, about 8 per cent of the city, then 1,541 in 1749 and only 478 in 1797. Jean Gelman Taylor presents Batavia as a trading base that grew into the centre of the Dutch Asian empire, where Europeans and Asians changed one another.

> More
Vink's tables separate "Europeans" from "mixed peoples" but never define either. Batavia's surrounding districts (Ommelanden) held only 600–800 Europeans and mestizos together. Vink counts settlements, not the whole VOC world, and gives no grand total of Europeans.

## 1600s–1800s · A mixed society
```yaml
year: [1632, 1800]
camera: [-6.5, 107.5, 7]
places:
  - { name: Batavia, at: [-6.13, 106.81], kind: city, note: "Taylor's case study of a hybrid Indo-Dutch elite" }
sources:
  - taylor2009: "publisher description only"
  - bosma2008: "as reported in Van der Veur's review"
  - vanderveur2008: "pp. 187, 190"
  - bosma2012: "ch. 3 (Laarman), p. 52"
evidence: contested
```
Few European women travelled to the Indies, so many men formed families with Asian women. Laarman reports 220 European women per 1,000 men in 1632, against 884 in 1930. Taylor argues this produced a hybrid Indo-Dutch culture. Bosma and Raben are reported to say Indos made up almost 80 per cent of "Europeans", but their reviewer notes that this counts creoles broadly.

> More
Bosma and Raben trace "creolisation" from 1500 to 1920. Van der Veur criticises some of their claims, for example about European women in the seventeenth century. Laarman adds that 20–25 per cent of European marriages in the late colony were mixed (p. 51).

Van Imhoff and Beets cite a count of about 2,000 Europeans on Java and Madura in 1814, not counting the military. > More

## 1854–1930 · Who counted as European?
```yaml
year: [1854, 1930]
camera: [-2.5, 112.0, 4]
places:
  - { name: Batavia, at: [-6.13, 106.81], kind: city, note: "Register of all Europeans kept by the Justice department" }
sources:
  - linde2024: "pp. 120, 124–126"
  - imhoff2004edu: "pp. 336, 341, 345"
  - nidi2002: "p. 22"
  - yoshida2008: "abstract"
```
Colonial law, set out in article 109 of the 1854 constitution, split Europeans from everyone else, and let the state move individuals across the line. Japanese residents gained European status in 1899. By 1930 the category held Dutch totok, Indies-born Indos, other Westerners, Japanese, Filipinos, Armenians, Turks, Egyptians and some Indonesians given equal status. A child recognised by a European father took his status.

> More
Van der Veur reports 7,195 Japanese and 542 Armenians among the 1930 Europeans, plus about 9,000 Indonesians who became Europeans by decree. Locher-Scholten calls the three-way legal split (Europeans, Foreign Orientals, natives) a form of legal apartheid.

The colonial census of 7 October 1930 counted 240,417 Europeans among 60.7 million people, 0.4 per cent. The NIDI report derived 86.7 per cent Dutch nationals from it (208,269 people). Of those, 73.9 per cent were born in the Indies and 25.8 per cent in the Netherlands. The 1920 census gave about 170,000 Europeans, though Van Imhoff and Beets call it experimental.

> More
Totals differ slightly: 240,162 for age and sex tables, about 243,000 with ship crews (NIDI), and 246,000 in Van Imhoff and Beets's Journal of Population Research article (p. 53). Locher-Scholten and Van Imhoff and Beets round to 240,000.

NIOD puts the pre-war European population near 290,000, with about 260,000 Dutch nationals; NIDI's model gives 294,000 to 305,000 in January 1942. > More
De Jong's regional table totals 96,400 internees; a Japanese report to the Red Cross gave about 98,000 and a postwar Dutch statement about 110,000. NIOD calls about 100,000 the most reliable estimate.

After Japan's surrender, violence against Europeans, Indo-Europeans and others swept Java and Sumatra. Estimates conflict. NIDI's demographic model assumes about 3,500 extra deaths; Van den Berge, citing Frederick, reports 25,000–30,000 Dutch and Indo-European civilian casualties. Captain and Sinke record local incidents and say about 46,000 people, 4,500 of them totok, were held in 398 camps on Java and Madura.

> More

Moluccan soldiers of the colonial army were not Westerners, but they travelled in the same exodus. Van Imhoff and Beets reconstruct 582,000 Indo-Dutch on 1 January 2001, counting the second generation: 458,000 in the Netherlands and 124,000 elsewhere. The total peaked at about 616,000 in 1984, with a margin of error near 10 per cent. The group is wider than the colonial category, covering European, Asian and mixed descent. In 1930 The Hague already had the largest concentration of Indies-born Dutch. None of the sources I read gives a count of Westerners in Indonesia today.
